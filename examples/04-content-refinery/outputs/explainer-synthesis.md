The Delphi Method
The Delphi method originated at the RAND Corporation in the 1950s through the work of Olaf Helmer, Norman Dalkey, and Nicholas Rescher, who sought a systematic way to harness expert opinion for forecasting. Its methodological core consists of assembling a panel of experts who remain anonymous to one another, soliciting their judgments in a series of iterative rounds, and providing statistical feedback—such as median estimates or interquartile ranges—after each round so that participants can reconsider their views in light of the group's consensus. The process typically begins with an open‑ended questionnaire, proceeds through rounds of individual response collection, aggregation of results, and dissemination of the summary statistics back to the panel, allowing each expert to adjust earlier estimates without seeing who said what. The sociological intent behind this design is to diminish dominance hierarchies, reduce the influence of assertive individuals, and foster a more egalitarian environment where consensus emerges from reasoned adjustment rather than persuasion.

Practitioners recommend the Delphi approach when the problem at hand requires expert judgment about future conditions, when sufficient time exists for multiple feedback cycles, when experts are geographically dispersed or prone to bias, and when empirical data are scarce or unavailable. Under these circumstances the method offers distinct advantages: it structures communication among experts, preserves anonymity to protect individuals from reputational pressure, and systematically reduces bias by isolating each participant's opinion before group influence is introduced. However, the technique also carries limitations that must be weighed against its benefits. It is inherently time‑intensive, demanding careful planning, facilitation, and multiple rounds of feedback; its validity hinges on the genuine expertise of the selected participants, making expert recruitment a critical step; and it is ill‑suited for urgent decisions or for issues that are not fundamentally forecasting‑oriented, such as prescriptive policy design or descriptive historical analysis. Consequently, acceptance criteria for employing the Delphi method include the presence of a forecasting need, availability of expert panelists willing to engage iteratively, sufficient temporal resources, and a context where bias reduction is a priority; avoidance is warranted when decisions must be made rapidly, when the problem lacks a forward‑looking component, or when the required expertise cannot be assured.
