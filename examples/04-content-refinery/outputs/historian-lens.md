# Historian lens (analysis) — completed

> Analysis (parallel): where does this method come from and how did it evolve?

SUMMARY  
- **Consultant 1 (Historian)** argued that the Delphi method emerged as a systematic, small‑group technique in the post‑World War II era, rooted in RAND’s work on expert panels. He stressed its chronological link to the 1950s RAND project and highlighted its military forecasting purpose, positioning Delphi as an early example of structured expert elicitation.  
- **Consultant 2 (Sociologist)** emphasized the method’s sociological underpinnings: anonymization, feedback loops, and controlled convergence. She pointed out that Delphi was designed to reduce domination by high‑status experts, to surface diverse perspectives, and to generate consensus without direct face‑to‑face interaction.  
- **Consultant 3 (Systems Engineer)** focused on methodological specifics: the iterative rounds, statistical aggregation of responses, and the explicit feedback of summarized results to participants. He noted Delphi’s influence on later consensus‑building tools in fields such as public policy and health technology assessment.

DECISION  
We will adopt an approach that **integrates all three perspectives**, presenting the essential origin story and evolution of Delphi in a concise, fact‑based narrative:  

1. **Historical anchor** – Delphi was created at RAND in the 1950s by Olaf Helmer, Norman Dalkey, and Nicholas Rescher specifically for military forecasting.  
2. **Methodological core** – It uses anonymous expert panels, systematic round‑by‑round feedback, and statistical aggregation to achieve consensus.  
3. **Social rationale** – Anonymity, feedback, and iterative convergence were deliberately engineered to mitigate bias, flatten hierarchies, and harness collective expertise.

REJECTED  
- **Purely chronological recounting** – Limiting the account to dates and names would omit the methodological and sociological innovations that define Delphi’s distinctiveness.  
- **Emphasis on later adaptations only** – Focusing on modern refinements (e.g., e‑Delphi, policy Delphi) would stray from the requested “essential origin story and evolution” grounded in the original facts.  

RISKS  
- **Historical inaccuracy** – Mis‑dating the RAND project or misattributing contributors could misrepresent the method’s roots.  
- **Oversimplification** – Reducing the sociological rationale to a single factor might underplay the intentional design to counteract expert dominance.  
- **Omission of methodological evolution** – Ignoring how Delphi’s feedback loops and statistical aggregation shaped later techniques would leave a gap in the evolution narrative.

ACCEPTANCE CRITERIA  
The deliverable will be considered correct if it:  

- Accurately cites the RAND origins (Helmer, Dalkey, Rescher) and the 1950s timeframe.  
- Clearly explains the key methodological components (anonymized rounds, feedback, statistical aggregation).  
- Highlights the sociological intent (bias mitigation, hierarchical flattening, consensus building).  
- Maintains a concise, integrated narrative that reflects the combined insights of the three consultants without introducing external or speculative content.
